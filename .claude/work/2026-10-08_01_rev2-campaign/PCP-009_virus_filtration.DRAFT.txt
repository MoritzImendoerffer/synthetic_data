1 Purpose and scope

The A-Mab drug substance process is being transferred from the development site in
Cambridge, MA, to the commercial drug substance site in Grafton, WI, under the Pro-
cess Transfer Plan PTP-001. Stage 1 process characterization supports that transfer
(U.S. Food and Drug Administration 2011). It establishes which process parameters
affect product quality, and it defines the ranges within which the commercial process
may be operated. This plan covers Step 9 of the drug substance train, small-virus
retentive filtration. The commercial process runs at a production bioreactor scale of
15,000 L.
The step is the dedicated small-virus removal step of the purification train and the
principal clearance step for minute virus of mice (MVM), a parvovirus. It removes
virus by size. Its clearance is therefore orthogonal to that of low-pH viral inactivation
(Step 6), which inactivates enveloped virus, and to that of anion exchange chromatog-
raphy (Step 8), which binds virus to the resin (International Council for Harmonisation
2023a).
The study covers both process parameters of the step, filtration volume (load) and fil-
tration pressure. It measures the log reduction factor (LRF) of MVM and of xenotropic
murine leukemia virus (XMuLV), the level of fragments and the step yield. It is run
in a scale-down model, on load representative of the anion exchange pool that feeds
the step at commercial scale.
The qualification of the membrane itself is held by the filter manufacturer and is
not repeated here. The cumulative viral clearance claim of the drug substance is
consolidated in the master report PCMR-001 across the steps that clear virus, and this
study establishes the contribution of Step 9 only. Process validation at commercial
scale is covered by the validation programme and not by this study.
The studies of this step ran under Version 1.0 of this plan, from 2026-04-15 to 2026-05-
15. Version 2.0 is an amendment issued after them. It restates the protocol of Version
1.0 with the changes listed in Section 1.1, and its criteria govern the analysis that the
report PCR-009 Version 2.0 presents. The deliverable of the study is PCR-009.

4 Prior knowledge and quality risk basis

4.1 Unit-operation description and prior knowledge

Virus filtration removes virus by size. The membrane has a distribution of pore sizes
chosen to retain the smallest viruses of concern, the parvoviruses, while the antibody
passes through. Retention is by sieving. It therefore depends on the integrity of the
pore structure and on how the effective pore size changes as the membrane fouls
during the batch.
MVM is close to the cut-off of the pore size distribution. Its retention depends on
the fraction of pores at the large end of the distribution and on the flow through
them. As product and residual impurity foul the membrane, the flow redistributes
toward the least fouled pores, which are the largest, and small virus can pass. MVM
retention is therefore expected to fall as the volume filtered per unit area rises. A
low transmembrane pressure, or an interruption of flow, can also let virus held in the
pore structure diffuse through the membrane. For this reason pressure is controlled
as well as volume.
XMuLV is many times larger than the pores. It is expected to be retained completely
while the membrane is intact, and its clearance to be insensitive to the operating
parameters. The post-use integrity test under the operation procedure SOP-2012
confirms that the membrane was intact.
The antibody is small enough to pass the pores freely. Step yield is limited only by the
product held up in the fouled membrane and in the system, and it is expected to be
high. Fragments are smaller than the intact antibody and pass the pores in the same
way. The step is therefore not expected to change the fragment level of its load.
The step is a platform operation. It was used for the three earlier platform antibodies,
X-Mab, Y-Mab and Z-Mab, and for A-Mab clinical supply (CMC Biotech Working Group
2009). The mechanism of removal is sieving and does not depend on the antibody.
Platform experience therefore supports the ranges chosen in Section 6.1 and the
expectation that MVM retention falls with load while XMuLV retention does not. The
platform history also gives the claim of this step, a log reduction of 4.62 log10 for
each model virus (master plan PCMP-001 §7.4).
Prior knowledge does not replace the study. The load of this step is the anion ex-
change pool of the A-Mab process, and how that pool fouls the membrane depends
on the product and on the residual impurities it carries. The maximum load and the
pressure range are therefore established on A-Mab material.

so it is measured and reported but judged against no criterion.



4.3 Risk-based prioritization of parameters

The risk assessment RA-001 ranked every process parameter of the train on its po-
tential impact on the quality attributes and on its potential to interact with other pa-
rameters (International Council for Harmonisation 2023b). Parameters ranked high,
or with a likely interaction, were assigned to multivariate study. Parameters whose
range could be supported one factor at a time were assigned to univariate study (mas-
ter plan PCMP-001 §4). Both parameters of this step were assigned to multivariate
study, and none to univariate study.

The filtration volume (load) sets how far fouling has progressed by the end of the
batch, and with it the share of the flow that passes the largest pores. It is there-
fore expected to bound the MVM claim. The filtration pressure sets the flux. A low
pressure lets virus held in the pore structure diffuse through, and a high pressure
compresses the fouling layer and raises the flux through the pores that remain open.
Both parameters act on the same population of large pores. The effect of one may
therefore depend on the level of the other, and both designs estimate the interaction
of the two.
The other operating variables of the step are held at their set-points under the oper-
ation procedure SOP-2012 and rest on platform experience (Section 6.4).

5 Materials and methods

5.1 Scale-down model and its qualification

The study will be run on a laboratory-scale filtration system fitted with the same mem-
brane type and membrane chemistry as the commercial step. The model is scaled on
membrane area, so that the volumetric load in L/m2, the transmembrane pressure
and the flux per unit area are the same as at commercial scale. The filtration pres-
sure is the transmembrane pressure across the filter. The load is matched because
the product and the residual impurity it carries are what foul the membrane, and
a cleaner pool fouls it more slowly. The filtration temperature is matched because
a higher temperature lowers the viscosity of the pool and raises the flux at a given
pressure. The hold-up volume per unit area is matched because it fixes how much
product is left in the membrane and the tubing at the end of a run, which limits step
yield.
The qualification study S-VF-SDM follows the scale-down model procedure SOP-1001.
The scale-down system will be run 3 times at the set-points of Table 6.1 on repre-
sentative anion exchange pool. The flux decay profile, the step yield and the post-
use integrity test result will be compared with the records of 5 manufacturing-scale
batches.
Under the offset rule of the master plan PCMP-001 (§7.1), the offset of an output is the
mean of the scale-down runs at set-point minus the mean of the manufacturing-scale
batches. It is reported with its two-sided 90 % confidence interval, each end of which
is a one-sided 95 % confidence bound. The model is qualified for an output when the
conclusion against its criterion over the NOR is the same with the offset at either end
of that interval. An output with no criterion at the step has its offset recorded only.
Step yield and flux decay have no criterion at this step, so their offsets are recorded
and no conclusion depends on them (Table 5.1).
The qualification runs will be made, and the offsets estimated, before the first screen-
ing run. The decision whether the model is qualified for an output is made at analysis,
because it needs the response-surface model of the step. At this step that decision
applies to no output with a criterion, because none is compared at both scales.
The two LRF and fragments are not compared at both scales. Their offset is unknown,
not zero, and they are judged as the scale-down model predicts them (PCMP-001 §7.1).
Two assumptions then carry the judgement, and the report PCR-009 will state each
where it is used.

  • Virus cannot be spiked into a manufacturing batch, so every log reduction
    claimed for this step comes from the scale-down model (International Council




                                          15
    for Harmonisation 2023a). Its representativeness rests on the design of the
    spiking model described above. The model uses the same membrane at the
    same volumetric load, pressure and flux per unit area, with matched load,
    temperature and hold-up.
  • The fragment result rests on the mechanism of the step. The clipped species are
    smaller than the intact antibody, so they pass the pores as freely as the antibody
    does, at either scale. The step is therefore not expected to change the fragment
    level of its load, and a scale-down result is taken to represent the manufacturing
    step.

5.2 Operation

The load is the flow-through pool of the anion exchange step. Each run will be oper-
ated at constant transmembrane pressure up to the volumetric load set by its design
point, and the filtrate will be collected in fractions across the load.
Before each run the filter will be wetted and its integrity tested. After each run the
post-use integrity test will be performed. Both tests follow the operation procedure
SOP-2012. A run whose post-use integrity test fails will be excluded from the analysis
and recorded as a deviation. A failed test means that the membrane was not intact,
and the log reduction measured across it then says nothing about the step.
Each design point will be executed once, with both model viruses spiked into the load
before filtration. Step yield will be determined from the same run by protein mass




                                          16
balance, from the ultraviolet absorbance of the load and of the pooled filtrate. The
filtrate pool of each run will also be tested for fragments. Runs will be executed in
random order within each design.
Filtration will be continuous. The claim made from this study applies to uninterrupted
operation, because an interruption of flow allows virus held in the pore structure to
diffuse through it. An interruption at commercial scale is a deviation and lies outside
the operating region this study defines.

5.5 Statistical methods

Both factors will be coded to the interval from −1 to +1 across the ranges studied in
Table 6.1, so that the estimated effects are comparable between the two factors and
between the two designs. Effects will be reported as the change in the response over
half the range studied.
The screening design will be analysed with a model of both main effects and their in-
teraction. The response-surface design will be analysed with the full quadratic model
in both factors (Montgomery 2017). Coefficients will be reported with their stan-
dard errors and p-values, and effects significant at α = 0.05 will be treated as real.




                                                   18
The screening model identifies effects. The response-surface model is the predictive
model, and every prediction and every range in the report PCR-009 comes from it
(master plan PCMP-001 §6.2).
Each fitted response-surface model will be reported with R², adjusted R² and pre-
dicted R², the model F-test, and the test for lack of fit against the pure error from the
centre points. The residuals will be plotted against the predicted value, against run
order and on a normal quantile plot. They will also be regressed on the predicted
value and on run order, and Cook’s distance will be computed for each run. Section 7
gives the criteria these statistics are judged against. The centre-point replicates also
give the reproducibility of each response, which will be reported as a coefficient of
variation and used as the pure error term.
Fragments are judged against their criterion on the prediction interval of a single
batch, because the criterion applies to each batch. The response-surface model pre-
dicts the mean response, so an interval is needed to judge a single batch. The inter-
val comes from a Monte Carlo simulation of the fitted model and includes the model
residual and the uncertainty of the fitted coefficients (Section 8). The two LRF are
judged on the mean predicted LRF at the least favourable combination of NOR low,
set-point and NOR high of the other parameter, because viral clearance is validated
at worst-case conditions (PCMP-001 §6.4).
Every judgement against a criterion is stated at manufacturing scale (PCMP-001 §7.1).
At this step no output with a criterion has an offset. The scale-down prediction is
therefore compared with the criterion without a correction, on the assumptions stated
in Section 5.1.
The analyses will be performed under the design of experiments procedure SOP-1002.
They will be run from a script under version control with a fixed random seed, so that
every number in PCR-009 can be regenerated from the raw data.

6.2 Screening design

The screening design of study S-VF-DOE is a two-level full factorial in both factors
with replicated centre points, 7 runs in total. Of these, 4 runs are the factorial points
at the corners of the ranges studied and 3 are replicates of the centre point.
Because the design has only 2 factors, the full factorial is run, and both main effects
and their interaction are estimated free of aliasing. The centre points detect curva-
ture but cannot attribute it to a factor, and curvature is resolved at the response-
surface stage. The interaction model leaves 1 degree of freedom for lack of fit, so
its fit statistics overstate how well it fits (master plan PCMP-001 §6.2). The screen-
ing runs establish the size and direction of the effects, and the reproducibility of the
assays at the centre point, before the response-surface runs are made. Appendix A
gives the planned coded and natural settings.



                                           21
6.3 Response-surface design

The response-surface design of study S-VF-RSM is a face-centred central composite
design in both factors, 12 runs in total (Montgomery 2017). It is made up of 4 factorial
points, 4 axial points and 4 replicates of the centre point.
The axial points sit on the edges of the ranges studied and not beyond them. A ro-
tatable design would place them outside those edges, at settings beyond the ranges
studied, for which the step may not be claimed. The face-centred form is therefore
used. The design supports the full quadratic model, which allows the response to
curve against the load. The response may curve there because fouling, and with it
the share of the flow through the largest pores, need not grow in proportion to the
volume filtered. A model limited to straight lines would then misjudge retention at
the top of the range, where the claim is decided.
The replicated centre point gives the pure error for the lack-of-fit test, with 3 degrees
of freedom against 3 for lack of fit. It also gives an estimate of the reproducibility
between runs that does not depend on the model. Appendix B gives the planned
coded and natural settings.

Where the study finds an effect that the two designs cannot separate, a univariate
confirmation run may be added. Any such run will be justified against the data that

7 Acceptance and decision criteria

Criteria apply to the scale-down model, to the fitted models, to the outputs, to the
ranges and classes that follow from them, and to the study as a whole.
The rule for the scale-down model is stated in Section 5.1. The screening runs will not
begin until the qualification runs are complete and the offsets are estimated. Whether
the model is qualified for an output is decided at analysis.
A response-surface model is adequate for prediction when all of the following hold.

  1. The model F-test is significant at α = 0.05, the level set for model terms.
  2. Lack of fit against the pure error from the centre points is not significant at α =
     0.05.
  3. Predicted R² falls short of R² by no more than 0.20. This is a limit on overfitting,
     because a model that fits its own runs much better than it predicts left-out runs
     has more terms than the data support.
  4. The residuals show no pattern against the predicted value or against run order,
     and no single run dominates the fit. A pattern is tested by a regression of the
     residuals on the predicted value and on run order, and a slope significant at α
     = 0.05 counts as a pattern. A run whose Cook’s distance exceeds 4/n, where n
     is the number of runs, is taken to dominate the fit. The cut-off 4/n is a common
     convention, and it falls as the number of runs grows.
  5. The normal quantile plot of the residuals shows no material departure from nor-
     mality.
  6. The residual standard error is small against the distance between the predicted
     response and its criterion.

Conditions 1 to 4 are those of the master plan PCMP-001 (§7.2), with the tests on the
residuals and on influence that every step plan applies in the same way. Conditions
5 and 6 are specific to this plan.
A model that fails any condition is not used to set a range. A model that fails the
lack-of-fit test or the limit on overfitting may first be refitted with a justified change
of terms or transformation (PCMP-001 §7.2). Where no adequate model results, the
report PCR-009 will present the response descriptively, against its criterion, over the
runs of the designs, and no range will be claimed from it.

8 Proven acceptable ranges (planned
  analysis)

A proven acceptable range is the range of a parameter over which an output meets
its criterion, given a stated treatment of the other parameter (International Council
for Harmonisation 2009). It is derived for each parameter and for each output with
a criterion in Table 7.1, which are the two LRF and fragments. Two analyses will be
run for each parameter and output, and both will be reported.

  1. The set-point analysis holds the other parameter at its set-point and scans the
     parameter of interest across its range studied on a grid of 201 points, using the
     fitted response-surface model. Its range is the contiguous interval around the
     set-point over which the mean prediction meets the criterion. It shows what the
     parameter may do while the rest of the step is operated at target.
  2. The NOR-propagated analysis scans the parameter of interest across its range
     studied on a grid of 81 points. At each point the other parameter is set in one of
     two ways, which depend on the output.

For fragments, the analysis draws 2,000 values of the other parameter at each grid
point. Each value comes from a normal distribution centred on the set-point, with a
standard deviation of the NOR width divided by 6, and is clipped to the range studied.
This assumes that the NOR spans ±3 standard deviations of the routine variation of
the parameter. Each draw carries the model residual and the uncertainty of the fitted
coefficients. The 95 % prediction interval of a single batch runs from the 2.5th to the
97.5th percentile of the simulated batches, and its upper end must not exceed the
criterion.
For the two LRF, the other parameter is set at each grid point to its NOR low, its
set-point and its NOR high. The lowest of the three mean predicted LRF must meet
the claim of the step. Viral clearance is validated at worst-case conditions, so a claim
that held only on average over the NOR would not support a validated log reduction
(master plan PCMP-001 §6.4).
The NOR-propagated range is the one that supports a claim. Where the two analyses
disagree, the range from the NOR-propagated analysis is carried into the control
strategy, because the commercial process holds each parameter somewhere within
its NOR and not at its set-point. The NOR-propagated range is therefore in general
the narrower of the two.
The reported range in each case is the contiguous interval that contains the set-point.
Where the prediction leaves the criterion inside the range studied, the PAR is nar-
rower than the range studied, and the report PCR-009 will state the value at which
the output crosses its criterion. A PAR that reaches the edge of the range studied is



                                          26
reported as reaching it, and no claim is made beyond it. Where an output does not
meet its criterion even at the set-point, PCR-009 will report that no PAR exists for
that output and will state the consequence for the NOR (PCMP-001 §7.6). Ranges are
derived only from a model that meets the adequacy criteria in Section 7.
Each PAR will be reported with a plot of the output against the parameter, with the
criterion drawn, the acceptable region shaded green, and the set-point and the NOR
marked. The two analyses will form the two panels of one figure, so that the cost of
the NOR-propagated analysis is visible. The ranges themselves will be reported in
PCR-009.

12 Risks and assumptions

The log reductions claimed for this step rest entirely on the scale-down model, be-
cause virus cannot be spiked at manufacturing scale. No output with a criterion is
compared at both scales, so the claim rests on the design of the model (Section 5.1).
If the model has to be changed, it will be re-scaled on membrane area and on hold-up
volume, the qualification runs will be repeated, and the change will be recorded in
the report PCR-009.
Spiking adds material to the load, and retention depends on how the membrane fouls.
A spike that fouled the membrane differently from unspiked process material would
bias the measured log reduction. The bias would be largest at the top of the volumet-
ric range, which is the part of the range that the claim depends on. The virus stock
will therefore be clarified before addition and its volume kept small against the load
volume. The flux decay of the spiked runs will be compared with that of the unspiked
qualification runs.
The log reduction that can be measured is bounded by the titre of the spiked load
and by the limit of detection of the infectivity assay. Where no virus is detected in the
filtrate, the result is a lower bound, and a lower bound that falls short of the criterion
cannot be resolved by repeating the assay. The load will therefore be spiked to a titre
that allows the criterion to be demonstrated with margin, and the achieved titre will
be reported with each run.
The study assumes that the anion exchange pool used in the runs represents com-
mercial material. Anion exchange is the step immediately upstream, and its own
characterization is reported in the anion exchange report PCR-008. Pool from a run
that was itself out of trend will not be used, and the load material of each filtration
run will be identified in PCR-009.
The judgement of fragments assumes that the step changes the fragment level of its
load at neither scale, which follows from the mechanism of the step (Section 4.1).
PCR-009 will state this assumption where fragments are judged.
The study assumes uninterrupted filtration, as stated in Section 5.2. The ranges it
produces do not cover an interruption of flow. An interruption at commercial scale
will be assessed as a deviation against the mechanism in Section 4.1, not against
these ranges.
Material supply is the principal risk to the schedule. Each design point consumes
anion exchange pool, and the runs will be scheduled against the availability of pool
and not against a fixed calendar.
